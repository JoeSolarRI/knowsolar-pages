Step 2

What Those Four Numbers Mean.

Batteries get sold on one number and fail on another. Here's the whole picture, in plain English.

kW vs kWh

kW is how fast power flows right now. kWh is how much you've stored. A 5 kW inverter feeding a 13.5 kWh battery is a small faucet filling a big tank.

Surge is the killer

A well pump or AC compressor pulls three to five times its running watts for a second. Too small an inverter trips on startup even with a full battery.

Usable is less than rated

A 13.5 kWh battery doesn't give you 13.5. Most systems keep a reserve to protect the cells, so plan on nine or ten usable.

Chemistry and cycles

Lithium iron phosphate (LFP) is the current default for homes: slower to degrade, safer, and typically rated for thousands of cycles.

Worth knowing

The Stuff That Actually Matters When You Buy.

None of this shows up in a brochure. All of it decides whether you're happy in year five.

Whole home vs critical loads

  • Whole home means your main panel gets backed up and nothing changes when the grid drops. It needs a bigger inverter and a bigger battery, and it's the only version that feels like nothing happened.
  • Critical loads means a subpanel with the fridge, heat, internet, and a few outlets. Cheaper, smaller, and perfectly fine for most people.
  • Load control is the middle path: whole home wiring, but the system sheds the water heater and EV charger automatically during an outage.

Battery or generator

  • Generator runs forever on fuel, needs yearly service, is loud, and does nothing for your bill the other 360 days.
  • Battery is silent, instant, no fuel, and earns something back every month. It carries hours, not weeks.
  • Both is a real answer for well water and heat pumps in a long February outage.

Things Joe checks before quoting

  • Panel capacity. A 200 amp panel with no space means a line-side tap or a panel upgrade. That's the most common surprise cost.
  • Where it goes. Garage, basement, outside wall. Clearances, heat, and code all matter.
  • Your actual load. Well pump size, heat pump tonnage, and whether the dryer and oven are electric.
  • Permit and inspection. Rhode Island towns differ, and the utility has its own interconnection step.

How a battery pays you back

  • Time-of-use arbitrage. Charge overnight when power is cheap, use it during the expensive evening window.
  • Demand response. Programs pay you to let the utility borrow your battery a few times a summer.
  • Rhode Island storage rebate. There is a state program for home batteries and it is first come, first served. Amounts and rounds change, so Joe checks what's live before he quotes rather than putting a stale number on a webpage.
Questions

Straight Answers.

Can a battery run my whole house including central AC?

Usually yes on paper, but it's a real jump in cost. Central AC is one of the biggest startup surges in a house. If cooling matters during an outage, the honest answer is a bigger inverter and a bigger battery, or a soft-start kit on the condenser. That's exactly why the calculator asks about AC separately from everything else.

Will a battery run my electric heat all night?

Rarely on its own. Electric resistance heat and electric water heaters are the two loads that eat a battery. Heat pumps are far gentler. Most homes in RI and MA end up backing up the heat pump or the gas furnace blower and letting the resistance strips stay off during an outage.

How long will it last in a real outage?

In a single outage, hours to a couple of days, and that depends entirely on what you keep running. That is the number the calculator above gives you in days at your own usage. Over its life, expect a home battery to be a ten to fifteen year purchase, with most current systems warranted around ten years or a set number of cycles, whichever comes first.

Do I need solar to get a battery?

No. A battery works on its own and charges from the grid. It's better with solar, because the sun refills it during a long outage instead of the grid staying down for both.

What about the state rebate?

Rhode Island has a funded storage rebate program and Massachusetts has its own. They change, they run out, and the amounts are per kilowatt-hour with a cap. Joe checks the current round before quoting, which is the only way to give you a number that's still true next week.

Why does your calculator ask about seasons?

Because a July outage and a February outage are different events. Summer is air conditioning and dehumidifiers. Winter is heat, and in Rhode Island winter usually runs longer. Sizing to the smaller season is how people end up cold.
